Using this in class

Three ways to listen

This atlas exists so that claims about music's universality stop being abstract. The exercises below pair the map with the week's readings: one on what songs are for, one on what songs share, and one on what happens in our brains when we listen together.

Exercise 1 · Functions

Can you hear what a song is for?

Turn on practice mode and work through 8–10 songs. Guess each function before revealing it. Which functions were easy — and which felt like coin flips? Naive listeners reliably identify dance songs, lullabies, and healing songs across cultures; love songs are far harder. Does your accuracy pattern match? What acoustic cues were you actually using?

Anchors: Mehr et al. (2018, 2019) — form predicts function across societies.
Exercise 2 · Universals & contrasts

Hunt for statistical universals

Pick one function and listen to it across four regions. No structural feature appears in all musical systems — but some are statistically widespread: discrete pitches, repeated phrases, isorhythm, octave equivalence. Which of these do you hear everywhere you click? Where does a song resist your categories — and is "music" even the community's own word for what you're hearing?

Anchors: Trehub, Becker, & Morley (2015) — universals, contrasts, and music conjoined with dance and ritual.
Exercise 3 · Listening together

One song, many synchronized brains

Play one excerpt for the whole room. When people hear the same natural music, their brain responses synchronize — in auditory midbrain and cortex, and in fronto-parietal attention and motor-planning regions — and this synchrony collapses when temporal structure is scrambled. As a group: what did everyone track together (the beat? phrase boundaries? a voice entering)? What does shared neural timing suggest about why musical rituals bond groups?

Anchors: Abrams et al. (2013) — inter-subject synchronization during natural music listening.

Data & sources

Where the songs come from

The recordings are 10-second excerpts from the Expanded Natural History of Song Discography (NHS2), an open corpus of 1,007 field recordings of vocal music assembled by The Music Lab and collaborators; this atlas maps a curated subset of 153 excerpts chosen to span all 10 behavioral contexts and all 39 world regions in the corpus (the excerpt IDs shown in the player are the corpus IDs, so any song can be traced back to the full dataset). Each song is placed at the geographic coordinates that Glottolog assigns to the language it is sung in — a language's point location, not a country: the corpus samples societies and communities, which rarely align with national borders. Coastlines are drawn from Natural Earth.
