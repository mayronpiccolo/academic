Part 1 · The elements of melody

Pitch is more than high versus low

Musical pitch is multidimensional. One dimension is pitch height — how high or low a tone sits, tied to frequency. The more musically important one is chroma — the pitch class a note belongs to (C, D, E…). Tones an octave apart share a chroma (octave equivalence), which is why the pitch space is drawn as a helix: height rises along the spiral while chroma circles back every octave.

1 · Height vs. chroma

Pitch helix

Play a note, then the same chroma one octave up — different height, same "family." Then climb Shepard's endless staircase: tones built to have clear chroma but ambiguous height, so the scale seems to rise forever without getting anywhere.

Chroma keeps cycling; height seems to keep rising.

2 · Intervals & contour

Melody sketcher

Draw a melody by clicking cells, then play it. Transpose it: every pitch changes but every interval survives — and it sounds like the same tune (relative pitch). Then keep only the ups-and-downs and scramble the interval sizes: contour alone preserves a family resemblance, but it's no longer quite your melody.

Most of us carry melodies as relations, not absolute pitches — which is why a tune survives being started too high, too low, or by a room that cannot agree on a starting note.

3 · Key: run your own probe-tone experiment

Krumhansl & Shepard (1979)

The context is a C-major scale that stops one note short — do re mi fa sol la ti, left hanging on the leading tone, exactly where Mendelssohn's visitor left him. Then you hear a single probe tone. Rate how well the probe fits that context, 1 (terrible) to 7 (perfect). Do all six and your ratings are plotted against the Krumhansl & Kessler tonal hierarchy: tonic highest, then dominant (5th)and mediant (3rd), then the other in-key tones, out-of-key tones last.

Part 2 · Time (a preview of the rhythm chapter)

Tempo, meter, rhythm

Melody rides on time. Tempo is the speed of the underlying pulse; meter is how those pulses are grouped into recurring strong–weak cycles; rhythm is the actual pattern of event durations laid over that grid. The demos below separate the three — notice how your mind imposes a meter.

5 · Meter

March or waltz?

Identical clicks. Accent every 2nd and it marches; accent every 3rd and it waltzes; remove the accents and — listen — your mind still tries to group the ticks itself (subjective meter).

Part 3 · What binds a melody together

Gestalt principles, in sound

A melody's tones arrive one by one, like loose beads — yet you hear a strand, not beads. Bregman called the sorting job auditory scene analysis, and argued the Gestalt grouping principles are its rules: proximity (in pitch and time), similarity (timbre), good continuation, and closure. Each demo below turns one principle up or down until the melody either fuses or falls apart.

7 · Proximity I: one stream or two?

Auditory streaming

Two tones alternate: low, high, low, high. When they're close in pitch or slow, you hear one bouncing melody. Pull them apart — or speed them up — and the sequence splits into two parallel streams. Find your own boundary.

Melodies worldwide favor small intervals — proximity is what keeps a line sounding like a line.

8 · Proximity II: Dowling's interleaved melodies

Dowling (1973)

Two very familiar tunes, note-about-note: A1, B1, A2, B2… Sharing one pitch range, they're an unrecognizable tangle — like reading ITNWTOEMRELLEOADVIEEDS. Slide melody B upward and, at some separation, both tunes pop out. Can you name them before you separate them?

9 · Proximity III: the octave-scrambled melody

Deutsch's "mysterious melody"

Violate pitch proximity and the tones "fly apart": most listeners cannot name a melody they have known since childhood. Guess first, then hear the octaves put back.

10 · Similarity: timbre takes over

Grouping by sound color

The tangled interleaved tunes again — same pitch range, hopeless. Now give melody B a different timbre. Similarity does what proximity couldn't: two instruments, two streams, two recognizable tunes.

11 · Closure: the note that wasn't there

DeWitt & Samuel (1990)

A rising scale, with a burst of noise near the top. Sometimes the expected note is under the noise; sometimes it's simply gone. Strong tonal expectations make the two nearly indistinguishable — your auditory system restores the note it was owed. (It's the Mendelssohn-in-the-bath principle: an unresolved leading tone begs for its tonic.)

Part 4 · When the principles betray you

Deutsch's scale illusion

In 1975 Diana Deutsch played listeners a C-major scale, ascending and descending at once — with consecutive notes of each line alternating between the ears. Each ear actually receives a jagged zig-zag. Yet not one of her 70 participants heard it that way: pitch proximity overrides ear of origin, and you hear two smooth arcs — the high one usually parked in the right ear. Grouping principles are so strong they can tell you the wrong thing about where sounds are.

12 · Hear it, then dissect it

🎧 Headphones required

Listen once and note what each ear seems to hear. Then audit the channels: play what each ear actually received. Finally, give the two ears different timbres — similarity now sides with spatial location, and the illusion weakens or collapses.

Most right-handers assign the high line to the right ear even when the headphones are reversed. Left-handers are more variable — ask around the room.
